Why Shared Amenities Are Becoming Part of the Residential Product

The resident experience begins before the apartment door. Arrival, amenities, and shared circulation now shape how a property is valued—and how it should be manufactured.

By Carve Creation · September 6, 2026

In multifamily development, the resident experience begins before the apartment door. Arrival, circulation, shared work areas, fitness and wellness spaces, lounges, outdoor areas, and everyday service points all influence how a property is perceived and used.

These spaces are no longer best understood as a collection of optional extras. They form part of the residential product—and, in mixed-use developments, part of the relationship between living, working, shopping, dining, and public space.

The 2024 NMHC and Grace Hill Renter Preferences Survey gathered input from more than 172,000 renters, showing how closely prospective residents evaluate both home features and community amenities. The implication for development teams is not that every property needs more amenities. It is that each shared space should have a clear purpose and perform well over time.

More space is not the same as more value

An amenity can photograph well and still underperform after opening. Spaces succeed when they support recognizable resident needs and fit the property’s location, resident profile, staffing, and operating model.

Before adding another room to the program, teams should define:

  • Who will use it and for what activity?
  • At what times and at what level of occupancy?
  • What privacy, acoustic, power, storage, or maintenance needs follow?
  • Can one setting support several compatible uses?
  • How will the space remain relevant as resident behavior changes?

This approach moves amenity planning away from a checklist and toward a deliberate experience strategy.

Shared amenities carry the property identity

Individual units may follow a controlled, repeatable standard, while common areas are often where a development differentiates itself. Furniture, lighting, signage, architectural features, and art collectively establish the property’s character.

That identity must also extend across different parts of a mixed-use project without making them indistinguishable. Residential, retail, hospitality, workplace, and public areas may need their own expression while sharing material cues, public-realm elements, or wayfinding logic.

A coordinated design language gives each use an appropriate character while making the overall development feel intentional.

Durability should be resolved without making spaces feel institutional

Shared environments experience more frequent and less predictable use than private interiors. Surfaces are touched, furniture is moved, signs are cleaned, and illuminated elements operate for long periods. Replacement access and maintenance become part of design quality.

The answer is not simply to specify the hardest available material. Durability depends on using the right material, construction method, finish, hardware, and detail for the expected use. Replaceable high-wear components, serviceable lighting, protected edges, and standardized hardware can extend life without compromising the intended aesthetic.

Full-scale samples and first-article reviews are especially useful when a custom detail will appear in several locations.

Repetition creates both efficiency and risk

Multifamily projects contain repeated components, but site conditions and unit or amenity variations prevent simple duplication. Mixed-use developments add multiple stakeholders, packages, and handover dates.

A controlled family of components can accommodate those differences. Standard materials, typical details, defined size ranges, and documented exceptions reduce repeated design effort and make pricing, production, replacement, and future expansion more predictable.

Phased release planning is equally important. Long-lead custom elements should be identified early, while location schedules and packaging plans should correspond to the actual installation sequence. This prevents the efficiency gained in the factory from being lost on site.

A better measure of amenity value

The most useful question is not “How many amenities does the project have?” It is “How effectively do the shared environments support the way residents live?”

Teams can evaluate that through several lenses:

  • 1. Relevance to the target resident and local context
  • 2. Frequency and diversity of use
  • 3. Contribution to property identity and leasing experience
  • 4. Operational and maintenance requirements
  • 5. Ability to adapt without complete replacement

When those factors align, shared spaces become durable components of the residential offer rather than short-lived marketing features.
